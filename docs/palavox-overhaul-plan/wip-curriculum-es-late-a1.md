# Palavox Overhaul — Spanish-for-English-Speakers Curriculum Plan: Late A1 Band (Units 4–6 + Checkpoint 1)

**Direction:** Spanish for English speakers (L1 = English, fixed; designed for anglophones from scratch — NOT the ESL course reversed)
**Band:** Late A1 — Unit 4 (Places & directions), Unit 5 (Food & eating), **Checkpoint 1**, Unit 6 (Shopping & money)
**Status:** draft (WIP) · **Author:** ES late-A1 band planner subagent · **Date:** 2026-07-30
**Regional policy (owner-fixed):** coherent broadly-useful Latin American standard — seseo, `ustedes` as the productive plural; `vosotros` receptive-only, labeled *(Spain)*; major lexical variants flagged (`carro/coche`, `computadora/ordenador`, `jugo/zumo`) without drowning beginners.
**Progressive-release ratios for this band (owner guidance):** 55–65% structured / 30–40% guided interaction / 5–10% open conversation

**Band headline numbers [proposed]:**
- **54 core lessons** (U4: 18, U5: 19, U6: 17) + **Checkpoint 1** (not counted as a core lesson)
- **455 new lexical items** incl. **92 multiword chunks** (U4: 145, U5: 162, U6: 148)
- **~31 hours** total active engagement (≈1,852 min median learner), split ≈61/32/7 across structured / guided / open
- **≥6 embedded AI interactions per unit** (≥3–4 spoken, ≥2 written) + 1 exit mission per unit

Labels used throughout: **[proposed]** = design decision open to revision; **[assumption]** = dependency on another band/team; **[requires validation]** = needs SME/data/native-speaker check before gating to `operational`.

- **[assumption]** The ES early-A1 band (U1–U3) delivers before this band starts: `ser` (full present), `estar` for greetings/feelings only (`¿cómo estás? — estoy bien`), `tener` (age, possession), regular present `-ar` fully + `-er/-ir` introduced, `gustar` intro (`me gusta + noun/infinitive`, singular only), gender/number and article basics, basic adjective agreement and post-nominal position, subject-pronoun drop as the norm, question formation by intonation + question words (with tildes shown), numbers 0–100, days/months/clock time, alphabet and spelling aloud, family/descriptions/daily-routine lexicon (~370 items), and first-pass pronunciation work on the five vowels, `ñ`, `ll/y`, silent `h`, and word stress. ## 1. Refined unit definitions

### Unit 4 — Lugares y direcciones / Places & directions ("Getting where you need to go")

- **Theme:** the learner's town and a Latin American city: naming places, saying where things are and whether they exist nearby, asking for/understanding directions from strangers, reading everyday signs.
- **Key language use:** transactional exchanges with strangers in public space; first sustained `usted` register use.
- **Content objective:** navigate to a named destination using spoken directions + a simple map; describe your own neighborhood.
- **Language objective:** `estar` (location) vs `hay` (existence); locative prepositions; `ir` + `al/del`; receptive direction imperatives (`siga, gire, cruce`); ordinals; `Disculpe…` politeness chunks; tap/trill r; stress-tilde literacy.

**CEFR anchors (Companion Volume 2020 descriptor language; exact scale placement [requires validation] against published tables):**
- *Understanding an interlocutor / instructions*, A2: "can understand simple directions on how to get from X to Y, on foot or by public transport" — U4 targets this **receptively** at slow-clear pace (the late-A1 on-ramp to the A2 descriptor).
- *Information exchange*, A1: "can handle numbers, quantities, cost and time"; A2: "can ask for and give directions referring to a map or plan" — U4's productive exit target, achieved **with support**.
- *Overall oral interaction*, A1: "can interact in a simple way… dependent on repetition at a slower rate of speech, rephrasing and repair" — the assumed interlocutor profile for all U4 AI speech.
- *Reading signs/notices*, A1: "can recognise familiar names, words and very basic phrases on simple notices in the most common everyday situations."

**ACTFL/NCSSFL modes coverage:**
- **Interpretive:** Novice High — follow short oral directions; identify facts on signs, maps, notices.
- **Interpersonal:** Novice High — request directions and locations using practiced chunks and simple sentences; request repetition/slower speech (`¿Puede repetir? Más despacio, por favor`).
- **Presentational:** Novice Mid→High — short written description of one's neighborhood; brief recorded "mi calle" talk.

### Unit 5 — Comida y comer / Food & eating ("Feed yourself")

- **Theme:** everyday food and drink, meals, likes/preferences, ordering in a café/restaurant, buying food at a market, prices.
- **Key language use:** obtaining goods and services; interpersonal exchange about preferences; courtesy formulas.
- **Content objective:** order a simple meal start-to-finish (including a substitution problem), state likes and wants, buy market items by quantity, understand prices aurally.
- **Language objective:** `gustar` extension (plural agreement, `a mí también/tampoco`); stem-changers `querer/preferir` (e→ie), `poder/almorzar` (o→ue); frozen courtesy conditionals (`quisiera`, `¿me trae…?`, `¿podría…?`); `tener hambre/sed`; quantities/partitives; `está rico` vs `es rico`; pure vowels + /x/.

**CEFR anchors:**
- *Obtaining goods and services*, A1: "can ask people for things and give people things"; A2: "can order a meal" / "can ask for and provide everyday goods and services" — U5's exit mission is literally "order a meal" with a supportive interlocutor.
- *Overall spoken interaction*, A2 (on-ramp): "can communicate in simple and routine tasks requiring a simple and direct exchange of information on familiar… matters."
- *Reading for orientation*, A1–A2: "can find specific, predictable information in simple everyday material" (menus, price lists, labels).

**ACTFL modes:** Interpretive (menus, server speech, prices); Interpersonal (ordering dialogues, market haggling-lite, plan-making by message); Presentational (short written dinner plan/review; recorded "qué como en un día").

### Unit 6 — Compras y dinero / Shopping & money ("Buy it, exchange it")

- **Theme:** clothing and everyday goods, stores, sizes/fit, comparing and choosing, paying, and low-stakes problems (wrong change, wrong size, doesn't work).
- **Key language use:** transactional service encounters with decision-making and the band's first **repair-under-pressure** work (feeding the owner's U12 repair strand).
- **Content objective:** complete a purchase end-to-end — locate item, ask about size/color/price, compare two options, pay, and politely resolve one problem.
- **Language objective:** demonstratives; `costar/quedar` third-person patterns; big numbers/currency; comparatives `más/menos… que`; direct-object pronouns `lo/la/los/las` (receptive → chunk-productive); b/v, silent h, seseo; question intonation.

**CEFR anchors:**
- *Obtaining goods and services*, A2: "can ask for and provide everyday goods and services"; "can make simple purchases by stating what is wanted and asking the price."
- *Information exchange*, A1→A2: "can handle numbers, quantities, cost and time" (prices to 10,000 in local currencies).
- *Asking for clarification*, A2: "can ask very simply for repetition when he/she does not understand"; *Compensating*, A2: "can use an inadequate word from his/her repertoire and use gesture to clarify" — the pedagogical warrant for 6.14's repair work.

**ACTFL modes:** Interpretive (ads, price tags, seller speech); Interpersonal (store roleplays, marketplace message thread, complaint exchange); Presentational (written marketplace inquiry; short recorded comparison "¿cuál prefieres y por qué?" at chunk level).

## 4. Vocabulary + lexical-chunk targets

**Counts per unit [proposed]:**

| Unit | Single-word items | Multiword chunks | Total new | Rationale |

**Selection principles [proposed]:**
1. **Frequency-first with a survival override:** default source = top-3,000 lemmas of a corpus-based Spanish frequency list (e.g., Davies *Frequency Dictionary of Spanish*) [requires validation: licensing]; items outside top-3,000 admitted only with a transactional justification (`talla`, `propina`, `recibo` earn their place; `berenjena` does not).
2. **Chunks are first-class citizens:** anything the learner needs faster than they can assemble it (`¿me trae…?`, `me lo llevo`, `es todo, gracias`) is taught as an unanalyzed chunk first, analyzed later (or never at this level). Chunks get their own FSRS cards.
3. **LatAm default, variants flagged not taught:** one productive form per concept (`jugo`, `papa`, `carro`, `computadora`, `mesero`); the Spain/regional variant appears once, receptively, in a labeled "variant flag" footer — never tested productively at A1.
4. **Gender is part of the item:** every noun is stored and drilled with its article; `el agua (f.)`-type exceptions carry a note field. English speakers have no L1 gender scaffold — the article is not optional metadata. **[English-contrastive]**
5. **Cognate leverage with false-friend quarantine:** transparent cognates (`restaurante, banco, mapa`) are batch-introduced at low cost; false friends in-domain (`éxito ≠ exit`, `ropa ≠ rope`, `sopa ≠ soap`, `embarazada` flagged later) get explicit contrast cards.
6. **Load ceiling:** ≤ 10 new items per teaching lesson core + ≤ 4 in interaction lessons; overflow moves to the "encountered, not owned" pool (receptive exposure logged, no productive test until re-introduced). **[proposed]**

## 5. Grammar progression across the band (ordered; each point tied to a communicative need)

All sequencing **[proposed]**; English-contrastive framing is the organizing principle — grammar is introduced when a transactional task fails without it.

| # | Point | Unit·Lesson | Communicative need it unlocks | English-contrastive note |
|---|-------|-------------|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| G1 | `estar` full present for location | 4.2 | Ask/say where anything is | Second "to be"; English has one — drilled as *location/state = estar* heuristic, extended in 5.13 |
| G2 | `hay` vs `está(n)` | 4.3 | "Is there a…?" vs "Where is the…?" | English *there is/it is* maps cleanly — leverage, then break the article habit (`hay un banco` / `el banco está`) |
| G3 | Locative prepositions + `del` | 4.4 | Understand/give locations | `de + el` contraction has no English analog; drilled as one word |
| G4 | `ir` + `a` + place, `al` | 4.5 | Say where you're going; destination talk | Suppletive `voy/vas/va` memorized as chunk paradigm |
| G5 | Direction imperatives, **receptive** (`siga, gire, cruce, tome, camine, doble`) | 4.7–4.8 | Survive real direction-giving | Formal imperative morphology NOT analyzed — frozen forms only; production deferred to B1 |
| G6 | Ordinals 1º–10º, `primer/tercer` apocope | 4.9 | Floors, streets, "the second left" | Apocope flagged, low-stakes |
| G7 | Stress + tilde literacy (`está/esta, papá/papa, dónde/donde`) | 4.14 | Read accurately; type intelligibly; hear stress | English has no diacritics — explicit keyboard + decoding training |
| G8 | Stem-changers: e→ie `querer, preferir` (4.–5.); o→ue `poder, almorzar, costar` (5.–6.) | 5.4, 6.4 | State wants, abilities, prices | Conjugation density shock managed: boot-verb pattern taught once, then instance-mapped |
| G9 | `gustar` extension: `gusta/gustan` agreement, `a mí también/tampoco` | 5.3 | Preference talk + reacting | Reverse-mapping (\*`yo gusto`) is the #1 tracked error (AGR-GUSTAR, §11) |
| G10 | Frozen courtesy conditionals: `quisiera, me gustaría, ¿podría…?` | 5.6 | Polite service requests | Taught as politeness vocabulary, NOT conditional morphology |
| G11 | `tener` idioms: `hambre, sed, ganas de` | 5.13 | Express states English does with *be* | "I *have* hunger" — direct contrast card |
| G12 | Prepositional pronouns: `para mí/ti/usted` | 5.6–5.7 | Ordering for people | `mí` tilde ties back to G7 |
| G13 | Quantities/partitives: `un kilo de, un poco de, mucho/a/os/as` | 5.11 | Market buying | `mucho` agreement — no English analog for quantifier agreement |
| G14 | Demonstratives `este/ese` (+ `aquel` receptive) | 6.2 | Point at merchandise | 3-way vs English 2-way; `aquel` receptive only |
| G15 | DO pronouns `lo/la/los/las`: receptive → chunk-productive | 6.9 | `¿Lo tiene en azul? Me la llevo` | Pre-verbal position is alien to English order; capped at chunk level this band |

## 6. Pronunciation progression (targets, minimal pairs, intelligibility rationale)

Philosophy **[proposed]**: target **intelligibility, not accent elimination**. Each unit has one [LAB] lesson plus micro-drills inside speaking exercises; ASR scoring thresholds are tuned per target [requires validation with pilot audio data]. English-speaker interference model drives selection.

| Stage | Unit | Target | Minimal pairs / drill items | Intelligibility rationale |
|-------|------|--------|-----------------------------|--------------------------|
| P1 | 4.6 | Tap /ɾ/ vs trill /r/; suppress English retroflex [ɹ] | `pero/perro, caro/carro, coro/corro, para/parra` | The contrast is phonemic; retroflex r is the strongest "unintelligible gringo" marker. Interim standard: a clean tap where a trill is due is **accepted as intelligible** [proposed]; retroflex is flagged. |
| P2 | 4.14 | Word stress + written accent as a decoding system | `está/esta, papá/papa, hablo/habló` (receptive awareness), `dónde, cuánto, qué` | Stress misplacement changes words and tenses; tilde literacy is also a writing-system skill (typing drills included). |
| P3 | 5.5 | Five pure monophthongs; **no schwa** in unstressed syllables; no diphthongization of /o/ /e/ | `no ≠ [noʊ], como, café, tomate, banana, chocolate, ensalada` | Vowel reduction is the highest-impact global intelligibility failure for anglophones — wrong vowels collapse Spanish's dense minimal-pair space (`peso/piso/paso`). |
| P4 | 5.5 | /x/ for `j`, `ge/gi` (`jugo, jamón, naranja, gente`); ñ (`mañana, señor`; `una/uña, sonar/soñar`) | menu-reading aloud drill | English /h/ for /x/ is usually intelligible → coached, not blocked; ñ/n confusion is word-changing → blocked. **[proposed]** |
| P5 | 6.6 | b/v = same phoneme (`vaso, barato, vestido`); silent `h` (`hola, hay, ahora, helado`); **seseo**: `ce/ci/z` = /s/ (`cien, cerveza, zapato`) | shop-talk read-aloud; `hay/ay` homophony noted | English /v/ for `v` is intelligible but marks; aspirated `h` actively confuses (`ahora` → \*[ahora]); seseo is the band's regional-policy anchor. |
| P6 | 6.6 + all U6 speaking | Question intonation: yes/no rise (`¿Tiene otra talla?↗`) vs info-question fall (`¿Cuánto cuesta?↘`); linking (`¿Cuánto‿es?`) | contrast-pair listening + imitation | Spanish has no do-support or inversion — intonation IS the question marker; flat intonation makes questions land as statements. **[English-contrastive]** |

Spiral rule: every [LAB] re-tests the previous labs' targets with 2 items; ASR error tags feed remediation categories PRON-R, PRON-VOWEL, PRON-HACHE (§11).

### 13a. Complete lesson — `es-5.6` "En el café: pedir con cortesía"

**Skill:** speaking (service register) · **Unit:** 5 · **Status:** draft · **Timing estimate:** 13–15 min
**`lessons.intro_md`:**
> En un café, la cortesía es parte del idioma. Hoy aprendes a pedir con **quisiera** (más suave que *quiero*), a pedir cosas con **¿me trae…?**, y a pedir la cuenta. El mesero te va a hablar de **usted**. Tú también.
> *Contrast note (EN):* Spanish makes requests with set courtesy formulas, not with "can I get…". `Quisiera` is a frozen polite form — you don't need to know why it works yet.

**New vocab mapped (`vocab_lesson_map`):** items #46–50, #53–54 of §4 sample list (`quisiera; para mí; la cuenta, por favor; ¿me trae…?; ¿qué me recomienda?; ¿cuánto es?; algo de tomar/comer`) + reinforcement of #1, #3, #12, #22 (`café, jugo, naranja, ensalada`).
